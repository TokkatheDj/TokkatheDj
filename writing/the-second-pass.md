# The Second Pass

*I re-read two of my own investigations looking for what the other side would say. I found nineteen
things to correct, and the worst one had been standing since July.*

This fall I worked on two pieces of public-records research. One is a documentary brief on how
federal institutions handled the Epstein case from 2006 to 2026, written for a reporter, a committee
staffer or a litigator to pick up and use. The other is a review of a grand jury report into a young
man's death on a Mississippi barrier island. Both are built from court filings, public laws, agency
statements and press coverage, and both cite every claim to a source.

As with my other projects, I built these with heavy AI assistance: an assistant retrieved the
documents, drafted the text and ran the checks, working from my direction. My part was deciding what
counted as evidence, and on 4 November, deciding that both documents needed a second pass by someone
arguing against them.

This is about what that pass found. Most of it was me, overclaiming.

---

## 1. The brief had a question it never searched for

The Epstein brief turns on a 2007 non-prosecution agreement. That agreement included a clause
promising not to prosecute "potential co-conspirators", and a 2019 court ruling declined to decide
what that clause was worth, because the people it covered weren't in the case. From July on, the
brief treated that as open. Section VIII listed whether the clause reached beyond Florida as
**"Unknown,"** and the records requests drafted alongside it asked about it.

It wasn't unknown. Ghislaine Maxwell raised the clause as a defense, and on **17 September 2024**
the Second Circuit rejected it: "the NPA with USAO-SDFL does not bind USAO-SDNY" (*United States
v. Maxwell*, No. 22-1426). The Supreme Court declined to review the case on 6 October 2025. That
ruling binds only one circuit, and the court noted that circuits disagree, so the question isn't
closed everywhere. But "no court has tested this" was simply false, and it had been false the whole
time.

The correction log calls it the brief's "most serious error of fact." How it got missed matters more
than the error. Every search the project ran was for the record it already believed in: dockets
about the agreement, filings by survivors, the Department's own reports. No search looked for the
case where a defendant used the clause and lost, because that is a win for the prosecution, and the
brief was a case against the prosecutors. The answer had been public since 2024.

## 2. The rest of the headline was overstated too

Once the pass was looking from the other side, more of the headline came apart. The 4 November entry
in the brief's correction log lists **eleven** items. The largest:

- It said wrongdoing was **"formally established four separate times."** The Justice Department's
  internal review found no professional misconduct, and Congress made no finding. The brief also
  left out that Epstein was charged in 2019 and Maxwell convicted.
- Its boldest line was **"The government withheld the names of powerful men and published the
  identities of the children."** The second half is documented. The first never was, and by October
  the only redactions tested in court had gone the Department's way.
- It set a congressman's "DOJ is complying" against a federal judge as positions on "the same
  question." His statement was from 18 August 2025, about a House subpoena, before the law the judge
  was enforcing existed.
- It said a violation was "conceded in open court." It was a default in a written filing, as the
  brief's own Section V explained.

The other six were smaller: a word like "sworn" or "published" the source didn't support, a count
the record didn't establish, a 2007 date off by two days. The thesis, one heading, one request and
every sentence built on those claims were rewritten, and the brief tells anyone holding an earlier
copy to replace it.

## 3. The review leaned the same way, more quietly

The Mississippi review had already been through one honest correction. It began from the question
"did they go easy and then go quiet?", and on 1 November its log recorded that the starting
hypothesis "did not survive the record." The grand jury report answered the first half: not on this
record.

The second pass still found **eight** corrections in the 1 November draft. It said the grand jury
"found no credible evidence of a crime," but the report's sentence is narrower: it concerns the
public allegations against particular people. It said "at least five" were charged, when sources could be
opened for four. It said the report "redacts every name," when it leaves some unredacted.

The useful part is how the log sorts its omissions. One entry is labelled **"Left out, against the
review's own conclusion"**: the family's claim that a phone was wiped, and the District Attorney's
answer. The next is labelled **"Left out, in the institutions' favor"**: where a phrase in the judge's
charge actually came from, and testimony about a fall. A draft that has given up its starting
suspicion can still lean on what it picks to include. The labels are there so the next reader can
see which way it leaned.

## 4. It had spread into the summaries

The same overclaims had already been copied into the documents that described this work to someone
else: an evidence log, interview notes and a cover letter for an application. Two lines there were
false in a way that would have been easy for a reader to check:

- **"Every press source was opened before it was cited."** Two hadn't been on 1 November, because
  the site refuses automated access, and one still hasn't. The review itself marks it "identified,
  not opened." The summary had rounded that away.
- **"Names no victim."** The brief names two survivors, both of whom testified or spoke publicly and
  are quoted from that public record. The rule I actually hold is narrower: no one who asked not to
  be identified, and nothing about a living person beyond what documents state.

Both were fixed wherever they appeared, with a line to use if an old version had already gone out:
"On a second pass I found I'd overstated that, and corrected it."

## 5. The checks I had couldn't see this

The brief's sourcing is checked by machine. A script pulls every quotation out of the brief and
looks for it in the extracted text of the archived sources. After the corrections it counted **169**
quotations, found **118**, and could not place **51**. On inspection those were mostly quotes from
press articles that aren't archived, and pieces of the brief's own text the script took for
quotations. They still need a human read.

But the script never had a chance at the eleven. The Maxwell error wasn't a misquote; it was a
search nobody ran. "Formally established four separate times" quoted nothing. Every overclaim in the
summaries was a sentence about the work, not a quotation from a source. A checker can tell you that
the words in quotation marks exist. It can't tell you that the sentence around them is fair.

That's the pattern in the project's own history, and it's the one recorded as its standing lesson.
Earlier corrections, on 26 and 29 July, caught **the press** inverting what court documents said:
that the Department "ignored" a deadline it had in fact answered, and a protective order described
as a warning to an official who wasn't a party to the case. Those were satisfying to find. The
4 November log ends by admitting the project "kept correcting the press and the Department more
readily than itself."

## What I took from it

**Search for the other side's wins.** The record you already have was gathered by your question. The
evidence against you is in the cases you'd be sorry to find, so look there on purpose: who used this
clause, and what happened to them?

**A finding is worth what survives its strongest critic.** Both documents got narrower, and both are
more useful for it. A reporter can build on a narrower claim. A broad one gets the whole document
dismissed when the first reader checks it.

**Correct the summary, not just the source.** The worst place for an overclaim is the one-paragraph
version, because that is the only part most people read, and it is the first thing a skeptic tests.

This carries into teaching. A student checking their own work looks for what they got right; the
same student checking a partner's finds the mistakes straight away. The habit worth teaching is the
second pass turned on yourself: before defending an answer, write down what a classmate would say is
wrong with it, and go looking for that. It's slower, and it's the step that catches what you'd
otherwise defend.
